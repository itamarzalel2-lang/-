# Alone in Orbit Ep 01: Day 1 to Day 365

14 parts of 1.5–2 min (2 uploads a day for a week). Each part stops at its most interesting
moment; the next part opens on the last ~3 seconds of the previous one. First person, one
narrator (Kokoro voice 1: `bm_fable*0.6+am_michael*0.4`, en-us, speed 1.06). Tone: suspense
and mystery, no gore, no jump scares. The ending is fully closed. The explanation is human and
realistic: no aliens, no ghosts.

Parts live in `videos/orbit-01` … `videos/orbit-14`; shared drawing code in `engine/lib/toon.js`.

## People
- **Me: Sam Cole**, flight engineer. Adult, calm, practical. Dark messy hair.
- **Commander Hale**: gray flat-top, mustache. Wrote the three rules. Was ordered to run the study, hated it, and hid the truth in his envelopes.
- **Ortiz**: pilot, curly hair. Leaves with Hale on Day 1. Believes the leak was real.
- **Mara Lind**: mission specialist on the other half of the station. Ponytail. Was told the same story from the other side.
- **Larkspur Orbital**: the (fictional) company that owns Larkspur Station and runs Project SOLO.
- **Inés**: a radio amateur in Chile who hears them on Day 300.

## The rules (Hale's letter, Day 1)
> Three rules.
> 1. Never open Hatch 3. Not for anyone. Not even for me.
> 2. Every morning, check three numbers. Oxygen. Water. Power.
> 3. Talk to Ground only on channel 4. If anything calls on channel 9, don't answer.
>
> Twelve envelopes. One a month. Never early. — Hale

## The truth (revealed by Part 12, never contradicted before)
Larkspur Station has two halves joined at Hatch 3, three crew on each side. Larkspur Orbital is
secretly testing how a person copes with a year of total isolation (to sell the data to a Mars
program). On Day 1 a fake ammonia-leak alarm sounds in both halves. Each half evacuates in its
own capsule. Each capsule has one seat tagged NO GO, so one person stays on each side: Sam on
side A, Mara on side B. Each is told the other half was vented and everyone there is "gone".
The pressure gauges on both sides of Hatch 3 are fakes, wired to read zero. Ground answers
only "Ground copies. Continue." Channel 9 is the station's internal intercom; the company uses it
to test whether they obey ("This is Ground. Open Hatch 3."). Hidden cameras watch both. Hale was
ordered to write the rules. He made rule 2 (count the numbers) and the envelopes his way of
letting them find each other: two people breathe twice the oxygen.

## Clues planted in Part 1
- "Everyone on that side is gone." (gone = evacuated, not dead)
- The capsule's fourth seat has a red NO GO tag (deliberate).
- The Hatch 3 gauge reads zero (it's a fake).
- Oxygen drops twice as fast as it should for one person (Mara is breathing it too).
- "Not even for me." (paid off on Day 365)

## Real-world details used
- A low orbit like the ISS's is ~400 km (~250 miles) up, one lap ~90 minutes (NASA ISS facts: https://www.nasa.gov/international-space-station/space-station-facts-and-figures/).
- Oxygen per person per day: published figures run 0.8–0.98 kg (0.84 kg in NASA-STD-3001/BVAD-based tables, 0.816 kg in life-support calculators, 0.98 kg in an MIT study), so Part 1 says "less than one kilo" and the tank losing 1.7 kg a day is about two people. Sources: https://apu.edu/files/cris_lunar_life.pdf, https://lifesupportcalc.neocities.org/, https://web.mit.edu/12.000/www/finalpresentation/environment/awn.html
- High CO2 on a station causes headaches and confusion; a scrubber removes it (Part 9–10).
- Ham radio on the ISS lets astronauts talk to people on the ground (ARISS: https://www.ariss.org/) (Part 12–13).
